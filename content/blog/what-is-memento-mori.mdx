You already know you will die. The trouble is that you know it the way you know the capital of a country you have never visited. The fact is in a drawer. **Memento mori** is the practice of taking it out while you still have a morning left.

The Latin is two words. *Memento*: remember. *Mori*: to die. English speakers usually hear "remember you will die" or "remember you must die." Either rendering is fine. The phrase is a command, not a caption.

## The history, without the postcard version

People like to say that a slave stood behind a Roman general during a triumph and whispered "memento mori" in his ear. That story is almost right and slightly wrong, which is how most useful stories travel.

Ancient writers describe a check on glory. Tertullian, writing centuries after the Republic, has a companion in the chariot telling the victor to look behind him and remember that he is a man: *respice post te, hominem te memento*. Other versions put a similar warning in the mouth of a public slave. The two-word slogan you see on rings and tattoos is a later compression. The Romans were doing the thing. Later Europe named the sticker.

Medieval and early modern Europe then put the reminder into objects: tombs with decaying portraits, hourglasses, snuffed candles, a skull on a scholar's desk. Dutch still-life painters called the genre vanitas. Fruit, flowers, a watch, bone. The painting is pretty. The argument is not. Everything in the bowl is on a timer, including the person looking at the bowl.

Stoicism sits in the middle of that long habit. The school starts in Athens with Zeno and becomes, in Rome, a practical ethics for people with jobs, families, and emperors. The [famous Stoics](/blog/famous-stoics-on-mortality) do not use the phrase "memento mori" as a brand. They keep circling the same fact: you could be gone tonight, and most of what you are clutching will not come with you.

## What the practice is for

A mortality reminder that does not change a decision is just interior decorating. The Stoic use is narrower.

You remember death in order to:

1. **Stop negotiating with later.** Later is where people store the apology, the book, the visit, the honest conversation. Later is not a place. It is a wish with a calendar.
2. **Put a price on attention.** An evening of nothing in particular is allowed. An evening of nothing in particular that you will still resent on the last week is expensive.
3. **Loosen the grip on things that are not yours.** Reputation, other people's moods, the next quarter's luck. Death makes a lot of status look like rented furniture.
4. **Take the work in front of you seriously.** Not solemnly. Seriously. If this is one of a few thousand weeks, the craft of the afternoon is not a side quest.

Marcus Aurelius writes, in so many words, that you could leave life right now, and that this should shape what you do, say, and think (*Meditations* 2.11). He is not trying to frighten a child. He is trying to run an empire without pretending he has surplus centuries.

Seneca, in *On the Shortness of Life*, refuses the complaint that life is too short. The span is long enough, he says, if you do not throw it away. People are frugal with money and drunk with hours. That sentence still works if you have never heard of Nero.

## What it is not

It is not a dare to be reckless. The Stoics were not asking you to ride without a helmet because "we all die anyway." They were asking you to stop wasting the ride.

It is not a personality. A skull on a laptop does not mean you have faced anything. You can buy the aesthetic and still live as if the sequel is guaranteed.

It is not a medical prognosis. A [life-in-weeks calendar](/blog/life-in-weeks) that runs to 80 years is a scale, not a promise. Some people get more weeks. Plenty get fewer. The practice still holds if the grid is shorter than you hoped. In that case it holds harder.

It is not an argument that life is meaningless. The move is the opposite. If the run is finite, the scenes are not interchangeable. Meaning gets sharper when you cannot reprint the day.

## How it shows up in ordinary life

The cleanest modern version is the question [Steve Jobs said he asked every morning](/blog/steve-jobs-memento-mori): if today were the last day of your life, would you want to do what you are about to do? He asked it for decades before he was ill. The diagnosis later made the question less abstract. The useful part is that you can ask it while you are healthy, which is when most people refuse.

A quieter version is a grid. You look at the weeks already inked in. You look at the blank ones. You ask whether the thing you are about to start deserves a cell. That is what this site is for. The philosophy is older than the browser. The browser just makes the grid easy to keep.

If you want something you can do this week without buying a ring or quoting Marcus at dinner, use the [practice note](/blog/how-to-practice-memento-mori). The short list is: a morning question, a weekly look at the calendar, and a refusal to treat death as a vibe.
