[Memento mori](/blog/what-is-memento-mori) fails in two directions. Some people never do it. Some people turn it into a hobby of gloom. The usable middle is smaller than the internet suggests: a few questions, a picture of the span, and a bias toward the next honest action.

You do not need a skull ring. You need a way to remember, on an ordinary Tuesday, that the Tuesday counts.

## 1. The morning question, sized for a workday

[Steve Jobs](/blog/steve-jobs-memento-mori) asked whether he would want today if it were his last. That is a good question and a large one. If you ask it about your entire biography before coffee, you will get poetry and no leverage on 9:40am.

Ask it about the next three hours.

If the answer is yes, start. If the answer is no, change one thing you actually control: the meeting, the errand, the way you speak to the person in the kitchen. Do not demand a new life before the first obligation. Jobs himself treated a string of "no" days as a signal to change course, not as a license to detonate Monday.

Write the question on a card if you like objects. Put the card where you already look. The practice is the asking, not the stationery.

## 2. Look at a life in weeks once a week

A [life calendar](/blog/life-in-weeks) is a bad daily ritual. You will numb to it. It is a good weekly one.

Pick a time that already exists: Sunday evening, Friday after work, the first coffee of the week. Open the grid. Spend three minutes. Ask only this: what deserves a cell next week, and what is currently stealing cells without earning them?

Eighty years is 4,160 weeks. You will not feel that number in a paragraph. You will feel it when a few thousand boxes are already dark.

If you live with someone, you can look at the overlapping weeks you still have together. That sounds theatrical until you do it once. Then it usually becomes a phone call.

## 3. Premeditation without catastrophe theatre

The Stoics practiced *premeditatio malorum*: imagining losses before they arrive, so that you are less stupid when they do. Seneca is the loudest on this. The point is not to spend the afternoon rehearsing plane crashes.

Keep it local.

- Imagine that the project ships and nobody cares. Would you still respect the work?
- Imagine that the person you are angry at is gone. Is the anger still worth the rest of the day?
- Imagine that you do not get the extra decade you are counting on. What moves up the list?

Stop when you have a decision. If the exercise produces only dread, you are not doing premeditation. You are chewing. Close it. Go to the next physical task. Epictetus would send you to lunch.

## 4. A death that is not yours, treated carefully

Some of the old exercises ask you to remember that other people will die, including the ones you love. That can be clarifying. It can also be a way to hurt yourself on purpose and call it philosophy.

If you are already raw, skip this one. If you are numb with busyness, a short version is enough: this person is not a fixture. Say the true thing. Book the visit. Leave the rest of the thought alone.

Marcus writes as someone who buried children. He does not treat mortality as a thought experiment. Neither should you.

## 5. Let the reminder change a small no

The test of the practice is not how you feel during it. The test is a refusal you can point to.

No to a commitment you were going to accept out of vanity. No to another hour of a thing you already know is empty. No to postponing an apology because you are still in the right.

[Seneca](/blog/famous-stoics-on-mortality) is brutal on occupied emptiness: a full schedule that contains no life. A small no is how you cut a hole in that schedule and put a life through it.

## What to skip

**Daily quote mail.** If a maxim in your inbox changed people, the Stoics would have ended the school after the first letter.

**Public performance.** Posting about death is not remembering death. It is content. Sometimes the content is fine. It is still not the practice.

**Using memento mori as a mood.** If the thought of dying makes you cruel, grand, or paralyzed, you have picked up the wrong end of the tool. The [Stoic](/blog/famous-stoics-on-mortality) aim was steadier judgment, not a darker personality.

**Pretending the grid is a productivity system.** Filling remaining weeks with optimization is how you get a life that looks full in a tracker and thin in a room. Some cells should be wasted on purpose, with people, on work that will not scale.

## A week you can actually run

- **Most mornings:** three-hour last-day question. Then work.
- **One evening:** three minutes on the [calendar](/). One sentence in a notebook: what the next week is for.
- **When you are stuck:** the local premeditation. One imagined loss. One decision. Stop.

That is enough. If you want the history under the habit, read [what memento mori means](/blog/what-is-memento-mori). If you want the names, read the [Stoics](/blog/famous-stoics-on-mortality). If you only do one thing, do the grid. The rest of the tradition is a commentary on a count you can already see.
