# The Simple Path to Wealth

**An editorial update of a spoken summary of J.L. Collins’s book**
**Facts checked as of 17 October 2026**

> Money is the single most powerful tool you have to create the life you want. It is not about greed or luxury. It is about freedom.

This document updates a spoken summary of *The Simple Path to Wealth*. The purpose, the argument, and the plain voice stay the same. What changed is anything that can go stale: law, tax brackets, account limits, fund fees, debt totals, and market levels.

Personal stories are the author’s account from the period of the book. They were **not** reinvestigated as a 2026 biography. Where a number could not be confirmed, the text says so.

## Opening

Money is a tool for a life you choose: leaving a bad job, helping family, traveling, or simply sleeping. Most people do not want a second career as portfolio managers. This book’s claim is that they do not need one.

- Spend less than you earn.
- Invest the difference.
- Skip products built to sound complicated.
- Let a broad market fund do the work.

Collins became known in the financial-independence community through his blog and this book. A claim that it has sold more than a million copies in 20 languages **could not be verified** here.

---

## The monk and the minister

A monk lives on little and answers to no one. A minister lives richly and answers to the king. The monk’s reply is the thesis.

> If you could learn to live on rice and beans, you would not have to please the king.

The less you need, the freer you are. A high income with a high burn rate can leave you stuck. A lower income with a high savings rate can produce independence sooner.

**FU money** is enough invested wealth to walk away. It is not a license to be cruel. It is options.

| Coworker | Pay | Lifestyle | Result in the parable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sarah | Higher | New car, downtown rent, dinners out | Paycheck to paycheck |
| Mike | Lower | Used car, modest home, high savings rate | On a path to independence |

The names and salaries are a teaching sketch, not a 2026 budget.

## It was never about retirement

Collins traces the phrase *FU money* to James Clavell’s novel *Noble House*. His own early jobs, a $5,000 trip in his twenties, later career breaks, and time at home with his daughter are autobiography.

The repeatable part is the method:

1. A high savings rate.
2. No consumer debt.
3. Low-cost index funds in the tradition of Jack Bogle and Vanguard.

Bogle died on **16 January 2019**, at 89. Vanguard’s structure and the index-fund idea survived him. [1]

Medicare still generally begins at **65**. A high-deductible plan can bridge a gap before that. It is also the door to a health savings account, covered in [Chapter 21](#chapter-21-health-savings-accounts).

Dollar targets in the talk ($5,000 saved, a $10,000 salary, a $50,000 stock loss) are his history, not a current budget.

## Important notes on numbers

Laws, brackets, contribution limits, and fund fees move. The concepts do not.

Projections in the original use **January 1975 through January 2015**. That window is when Bogle’s first retail index fund appeared, and when Collins says he started investing. The talk’s **11.9 percent a year**, dividends reinvested, is the book’s figure for that window. It was **not recomputed** for this update. Do not plan a life on it.

### A transcription error

The spoken line that 2000–2009 returned “93.8 percent annually” is a speech-to-text error. That decade was the lost decade: weak to slightly negative annualized returns, not a boom. The exact annualized figure was not recomputed here.

Other windows in the talk (a strong stretch from 1982 to 2000, and another from 2009 to 2015) only show that the average depends on the dates you pick.

### What happened after the book’s snapshot

Investors have also lived through:

- the 2020 crash and the recovery that followed;
- **2022**, when stocks and bonds fell together;
- a powerful bull market that, by late 2026, was still led by a handful of technology companies.

Averages still mislead.

### How to run your own numbers

Use a calculator and a modest assumption.

- [Investor.gov compound-interest calculator](https://www.investor.gov/financial-tools-calculators/calculators/compound-interest-calculator) is still published.
- [Firecalc](https://www.firecalc.com/) is the tool the talk mangles as “FireCalcom.”

An **8 percent** nominal figure remains a common round assumption. It is not a promise. Real returns are lower once inflation is included.

The book’s own sketches — about **8.7 percent** without reinvested dividends, about **4.7 percent** after inflation without reinvestment, and about **7.8 percent** with both — were not re-derived here. Treat them as teaching numbers.

## Chapter 1. Debt

Debt shrinks your lifestyle, ties you to a paycheck, and burns attention.

### The dollar totals moved

The talk’s “$12 trillion” picture is obsolete. In the **second quarter of 2026**, the New York Fed measured U.S. household debt as follows. [2]

| Category | Balance |
| --- | ---: |
| All household debt | $18.8 trillion |
| Mortgages | $13.1 trillion |
| Student loans | $1.65 trillion |
| Auto loans | $1.71 trillion |
| Credit cards | $1.26 trillion |

“Normal” is still not the same as harmless.

### A payoff order, not a statute

Collins’s interest-rate rule is a judgment:

| Rate on the debt | His suggestion |
| --- | --- |
| Under 3% | Pay slowly. Invest the extra cash. |
| 3% to 5% | Either path is reasonable. Pick the one you will finish. |
| Over 5% | Pay it off aggressively. |

Market yields in the mid-2020s are much higher than in the mid-2010s, so a true 3 percent loan is less common.

The practical order is still the avalanche:

1. List every balance.
2. Cut optional spending.
3. Pay the minimum on all of them.
4. Attack the highest interest rate.
5. When that balance is gone, roll the payment to the next one.

Do **not** pay someone to do this for you. Do **not** pay the smallest balance first just for a feeling of progress, unless that feeling is the only way you will stay with the plan.

### “Good” debt

- **Business loans** can be rational, and they can wipe you out.
- **A mortgage** can make sense if the house is the one you need, not the one a lender will approve.
- **Student loans** are the sharp edge.

Federal student loans are still presumed **nondischargeable** in bankruptcy under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8), unless the debtor proves undue hardship. Courts often use the *Brunner* test. A November 2022 Justice Department process, still posted with a **March 2026** update, makes agreed discharges easier in clear hardship cases. It does not turn student debt into ordinary credit-card debt. [3]

Whether defaulted federal loans are **currently** being offset from Social Security was **not reconfirmed** for this date. Do not describe the loans as disappearing at death. Do not describe them as impossible to discharge in every case.

> Debt is not your friend. It is not a tool for a free life. Every dollar aimed at high-interest debt is a dollar that stops working against you.

## Chapter 2. Why you need FU money

Collins’s layoff after 11 September 2001, and the conversation with his daughter, are his story. The point that survives is simple. Employers and lenders have more power when you cannot miss a paycheck.

A cash cushion plus invested savings is what lets you negotiate, wait, or leave.

Build it before the crisis:

- [ ] Save from each paycheck.
- [ ] Cut what you do not value.
- [ ] Refuse high-interest debt.
- [ ] Own the market, not a story about the market.

## Chapter 3. Can everyone retire a millionaire?

The math can work for a middle-income household in the United States. Behavior is why it usually does not.

### Historical, not a forecast

These examples belong to the book’s **1975–2015** window and its **11.9 percent** figure. They are not a projection from 2026.

| Starting habit | Book’s punch line |
| --- | --- |
| $12,000 once, dividends reinvested | Past $1 million by 2015 |
| About $130 a month | A large nest egg, not yet $1 million |
| About $150 a month | Past $1 million in that same window |

A new car is no longer the casual “leftover” those pages imagined. Car prices and the index both moved.

### Independence is the real target

Financial independence means investments large enough that work is optional.

At a **4 percent** withdrawal rate:

$$
\text{portfolio needed} = \text{annual spending} \div 0.04 = \text{annual spending} \times 25
$$

Spending of **$7,000** a year implies about **$175,000** invested. That example, from early-retirement writer Jacob Lund Fisker, is the book’s extreme. His **2026** spending was not checked.

The opposite extreme still fails: an enormous bonus spent as fast as it arrives. Wealth is what you keep.

### The $25,000 salary sketch

Halving a modest income and investing the rest can still produce independence. The year count depends on returns you cannot know in advance. Median wages are higher now than in the sketch, and so is the cost of housing, health insurance, and food.

Advertising still blurs needs and wants. That part did not expire.

## Chapter 4. How to think about money

A dollar can be spent once, or kept as a claim on future earnings. Collins’s picture of **$8 to $12** a year on **$100** is the same optimistic band as a high nominal equity return. Use it as a picture, not a quote.

Mike Tyson’s career earnings and his bankruptcy remain a fair cautionary tale about spending. Wealth is what you keep, and what you let the kept dollars earn.

### Opportunity cost

A **$20,000** car paid in cash avoids a loan. It also gives up whatever that $20,000 would have earned.

| Assumption | Rough result |
| --- | --- |
| 8% for one year | About $1,600 forgone |
| 8% for ten years, gains left invested | Meaningfully more than $1,600 × 10, because gains compound |

The car’s price in the example is dated. The idea is not.

### Own businesses, not a mood

Warren Buffett’s line — never lose money, and never forget that rule — is about avoiding stupid permanent losses. It is not a claim that the quote never falls. He lost enormous **paper** wealth in 2008 and did not sell the businesses.

A total-market fund is “self-cleansing” only in this sense: the index drops failures and adds new companies. You still own whatever the market owns, including stretches when a few stocks dominate.

As of **31 August 2026**, Morningstar showed Nvidia, Apple, and Microsoft as the three largest weights in **VTSAX**, with the top ten about **33.5 percent** of the fund. [4]

As of **17 October 2026**, Nvidia was still the largest U.S. company by market value, near **$5.2 trillion**, with Apple just behind, near **$5.0 trillion**. [5]

> “Apple is the largest” is no longer a stable fact. Rankings can change in a week.

The old illustration of buying VTSAX at **$53.67** is a past price. Vanguard’s site showed a net asset value of **$185.60** on **13 October 2026**. [6] You still own shares of businesses, not a feeling about this week’s close.

## Chapter 5. Bull markets and bear markets

“Early 2015, S&P 500 over 2,000” is a period detail.

| Index | Close on 16 October 2026 |
| --- | ---: |
| S&P 500 | 7,659.44 |
| Dow Jones Industrial Average | 52,002.41 |

Source: market closes reported for that session. [7]

The talk’s warning that people would call **5,000** “too late” is now history. They did. The index went through it.

From the mid-1990s to 2015 the S&P 500 did rise from the hundreds to above 2,000, through the dot-com bust and 2008. Extending the chart through 2020, 2022, and the mid-2020s does not remove crashes. It lengthens the same lesson.

No one, including Buffett, consistently sells the top and buys the bottom. A long investor should expect deep declines, sometimes on the order of half, more than once.

If you cannot watch a decline without selling, you are not ready for a **100 percent** stock portfolio. You can still start small, or hold some bonds, rather than wait for a certainty that will not arrive.

## Chapter 6. A crash is coming

These crashes are real:

- the 1970s inflation shock;
- Black Monday in 1987;
- the dot-com bust;
- the housing crash and Great Recession of 2008;
- the COVID crash in 2020;
- **2022**, when rising inflation and interest rates hit stocks and bonds at the same time.

The last item matters. “Bonds will save you this year” failed in 2022.

The Dow around **616** at the end of 1974, and above **17,800** at the end of 2014, matches the book’s sketch. The same index closed **16 October 2026** above **52,000**. [7] That is not a promise that the next 40 years look like the last 40.

### Rules that still hold

1. Crashes recur. You do not get to opt out.
2. In the U.S. record, recoveries have followed.
3. Over very long periods, stocks have beaten bonds, gold, and cash.
4. Behavior matters more than a clever fund.
5. When other people are selling in a panic, the patient buyer is being offered a lower price.

Crypto has had spectacular gains and spectacular losses since the book. It has not replaced a broad stock index as the core holding of this strategy.

## Chapter 7. Why the market has gone up

**Black Monday, 19 October 1987**, was a drop of about **508 points** and more than **22 percent** in one day. Collins’s account of freezing, then selling near the low, is the emotional center of the chapter.

“The market always goes up” needs cleaner wording.

- The U.S. market has recovered from every past crash in this record.
- It has risen over multi-decade stretches.
- It does **not** rise every year.
- Japan after 1989 is the standing counterexample for a rich country.

An index fund owns businesses. The worst business can go to zero. The best can compound for decades. The index replaces dead companies with new ones **only if you own the broad market**, not a single name.

The talk’s “about 3,700 companies” was **not reconfirmed** as a current holding count. The fund still seeks the whole U.S. stock market. [6]

Blockbuster versus Netflix, and the fade of older giants as Amazon rose, remain fair examples. Nvidia’s rise since the book was written is the newest version of the same story, and it is already a large part of the index. [4]

## Chapter 8. Why most people lose money

Three habits still explain most of the damage.

1. **Timing.** People buy after prices have already run, and sell after they fall.
2. **Stock picking.** A lucky pick gets mistaken for a repeatable skill.
3. **Manager picking.** The investor pays a professional to try, and pays whether the try works or not.

### What the scorecards say now

In **2025**, **79 percent** of active U.S. large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500. That was a poor year even by the history of S&P Dow Jones’s SPIVA scorecards. [8]

A secondary summary of those scorecards says about **90.5 percent** of U.S. large-cap funds trailed over the **15 years ending June 2026**. That longer figure was **not** opened in S&P’s own PDF for this edit. Treat it as reported, not re-audited. [9]

The book’s “only 18 percent beat the market in a 2013 Vanguard study” is an old study. The direction is the same. The citation to use now is the current scorecard.

### Foam and beer

There are still more products than an individual needs. Marketing shows the recent winners.

| In the glass | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Beer | The businesses: profits, products, reinvested capital |
| Foam | Daily prices, headlines, and television forecasts |

**VTSAX** and the ETF share class **VTI** remain the simple U.S. total-market tools. Fidelity’s comparable fund is **FSKAX**. The talk’s “FFSKX” is a mishearing.

## Chapter 9. The big ugly event

From the **1929** peak to the **1932** low, the Dow fell on the order of **90 percent**, from the high 300s to the low 40s, and took until the mid-1950s to regain the old peak in price terms.

| Situation | What history suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Bought at the exact top, with borrowed money | Ruin |
| Bought a couple of years earlier and held | A long, painful recovery, not a lifetime sentence |
| Kept a job and kept buying | The decline was also a sale |

The line that “75 percent of workers kept their jobs” was **not** rechecked against a primary Depression labor series.

### The other monster: hyperinflation

These examples are historically fair:

- Germany in the 1920s;
- Hungary in 1946;
- Zimbabwe around 2008.

The United States has not had that kind of currency collapse in the modern era. It **has** had painful ordinary inflation, including the early 1980s and **2021–2023**.

Stocks are a **partial** hedge, because businesses own real assets and can raise prices. They are not a perfect hedge in the year inflation arrives. Long nominal bonds suffered in 2022 for that reason.

> Never invest borrowed money in the stock market.

## Chapter 10. Three tools

Stage of life beats age. Someone at 60 may have decades ahead. Someone at 40 may already be spending the portfolio. The slogan “100 minus your age in stocks” is a rule Collins rejects, and he is right to reject it.

### The toolbox

| Tool | Ticker | Job | Fee checked for this update |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| U.S. total stock market | `VTSAX` | Growth | **0.04%** as of 28 April 2026. Minimum **$3,000**. [6] |
| U.S. total bond market | `VBTLX` | Smoother ride, income | **0.04%** as of 28 April 2026. [10] |
| Cash | Bank or money market | Near-term bills | Rate not checked. It will change. |

Vanguard’s own comparison, as of **31 December 2025**: [6]

- similar-fund average on the VTSAX page: **0.72 percent**;
- Vanguard’s asset-weighted mutual-fund average: **0.08 percent**;
- industry average excluding Vanguard: **0.50 percent**.

The book’s “industry average about **1 percent**” is no longer the right summary. The book’s **0.05 percent** VTSAX fee, and its **$10,000** Admiral minimum, are also stale.

### What cash is, and is not

- Bank deposits are **FDIC**-insured up to the legal limit.
- A brokerage account is a different protection regime. It does not insure you against a falling market.
- A high-yield savings account is a reasonable home for cash. Today’s rate was not checked.
- From 2022 through much of the mid-2020s, cash paid a visible yield again. That was not true when many readers first met this book.

Inflation is still the risk of “safe” cash. A balance that does not grow buys less later. The book’s “half as much in 20 years” assumes a particular inflation path. It is a scenario, not a measurement.

Bonds still damp **some** equity crashes. They did not damp 2022.

## Chapter 11. Index funds are not laziness

Bogle’s claim that he had not seen anyone consistently beat the market is his judgment from a long career. It is not a new statistical paper.

Two reminders from corporate history:

- General Motors was treated as too big to fail, and later needed a rescue.
- Apple was doubted in the 1990s, and by October 2026 was worth about **$5 trillion**. [5]

You still cannot know the next king. In 2026 the leading company is an artificial-intelligence hardware firm. Almost nobody in the original audience was underwriting that outcome.

### Fees eat the spending money

Once you are withdrawing about **4 percent** a year, a **1 percent** fee is a quarter of that spending money.

| Annual cost | Dollars per year on $10,000 |
| --- | ---: |
| 1.00% advisor or fund fee | $100 |
| 0.04% VTSAX | $4 |

Bogle’s line still earns its place: performance comes and goes, and fees are forever.

Fidelity, Vanguard, and Schwab remain sound places to hold a total-market index fund or ETF. At those firms, commissions on ETFs are generally **zero**. The book’s warning to hunt for a broker that does not charge a trading commission is mostly obsolete.

## Chapter 12. Bonds

A bond is a loan.

You can lose in three ordinary ways:

1. The borrower defaults.
2. Inflation eats the fixed payments.
3. Interest rates rise and you sell before maturity.

A total bond-market fund spreads issuer risk and maturity risk. It does **not** remove interest-rate risk. **VBTLX** is still the simple version Collins uses. [10]

### Tax flavor, in brief

| Bond | Typical income-tax treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Municipal | Often exempt from federal income tax, and sometimes from state tax |
| U.S. Treasury | Exempt from state and local income tax. Taxable at the federal level. |
| Corporate, inside a bond index fund | Taxable interest. Prefer a tax-deferred account if you have one. |

Whether a municipal bond wins **after tax** depends on your bracket. An inverted yield curve has often preceded recessions. It is not a trading signal this strategy uses.

A teaching example in the talk — a $1,000 bond at 10 percent — is arithmetic, not a bond you can buy in 2026:

- each year you receive $100;
- at maturity you receive the $1,000 back, if the borrower pays;
- if new bonds pay more, your old bond is worth less **on the market**;
- if you hold to maturity and there is no default, you still get the bargain you made.

## Chapter 13. Two portfolios

### While you are saving

Collins still argues for **100 percent VTSAX** if you can hold through a decline of 30 or 40 percent.

The “$200 a month from 1975 to 2015 became more than $1.5 million” case sits inside the book’s return assumption. It is history, not a projection that starts in 2026.

### While you are spending

His household example:

| Sleeve | Share |
| --- | ---: |
| VTSAX | 75% |
| VBTLX | 20% |
| Cash | 5% |

Rebalance about once a year. A more cautious mix, **60/40** or **50/50**, trades growth for a smoother ride. There is no perfect ratio. There is the ratio you will not abandon.

## Chapter 14. Choosing the mix

While paychecks cover your life, stocks fit. When the portfolio pays the bills, bonds start to matter. A **10 to 25 percent** bond sleeve is his mild suggestion for smoothing the ride.

Rebalance about once a year, or after a very large move. Bogle treated rebalancing as a preference more than as proven extra return. That is still a fair description.

### Target-date funds

A target-date fund does the glide path for you. You pay a higher fee than VTSAX alone. Exact **2026** target-date expense ratios were **not** pulled for this edit. The talk’s lineup “through 2060” is out of date as a ceiling. Check the current series.

The trick still works: pick a **later** date for a more aggressive mix, or an **earlier** date for a calmer one.

### Taxes when you rebalance

- Hold bonds in tax-deferred accounts when you can. Interest is ordinary income.
- Broad stock index funds are relatively tax-efficient in taxable accounts.
- The wash-sale rule still denies a loss if you repurchase a substantially identical fund within **30 days**.
- The **$3,000** capital-loss offset against ordinary income is longstanding. It was **not** re-read in the 2026 statute for this edit. Confirm it on the current Schedule D instructions before you file.

## Chapter 15. International stocks

Collins’s objection is personal, and it is still coherent:

- extra currency risk;
- looser accounting and governance in some markets;
- higher fees;
- large U.S. companies already sell worldwide.

Global markets are more correlated in a crisis than a textbook suggests. If you want a separate holding, Vanguard’s total international fund and the total-world stock ETF still exist.

The talk’s expense ratios, roughly **0.11 to 0.25 percent**, were **not rechecked**. Do not copy them onto a 2026 application.

A target-date fund will add international stocks whether Collins likes that or not.

## Chapter 16. Target-date funds

One fund. One retirement year. An automatic shift toward bonds. That is a legitimate simple path.

Vanguard builds these from:

- a U.S. stock index fund;
- a U.S. bond index fund;
- an international stock index fund;
- an international bond index fund;
- inflation-linked bonds as the date gets close.

Collins prefers separate funds, for three reasons:

1. The fee is lower.
2. He does not want a separate international sleeve.
3. He can place bonds in the tax-deferred account.

Both approaches are consistent with the book. Workplace plans often default you into a target-date fund. **Read the fee** before you accept the default.

## Chapter 17. If you cannot buy VTSAX

The **$10,000** Admiral minimum in the talk is wrong now. VTSAX’s minimum on Vanguard’s page is **$3,000**. [6]

The old Investor share class, **VTSMX**, is not the on-ramp it was when the book was new.

| Share class | How you buy it now |
| --- | --- |
| `VTSAX` | Mutual fund. $3,000 minimum at Vanguard. |
| `VTI` | ETF. No fund minimum. One share, or a fraction where the broker allows it. |
| Institutional class inside a large 401(k) | Often cheaper still. Confirm the ticker in your plan. The talk’s institutional tickers were not rechecked. |

If the plan has no Vanguard fund, take the match anyway. Then prefer:

1. a cheap total-market or S&P 500 index fund, or
2. a low-cost target-date fund.

When you leave the job, you can usually roll the balance to an IRA.

Outside the United States, copy the blueprint: a low-cost fund that **includes** the U.S. market, not a product that uses the word “global” while excluding American stocks.

## Chapter 18. Why Vanguard

Vanguard is owned by its U.S. funds, so the investors in those funds are the owners. That is still the structural difference from a publicly traded manager or a family-controlled firm.

It is **not** a promise that every Vanguard product is the cheapest forever. Fidelity and Schwab now match or beat it on some index ETFs. The reason to prefer Vanguard is alignment and cost, not a brand oath.

### Safety, stated carefully

| Event | What is protected |
| --- | --- |
| The market falls 40% | Nothing. That is the investment risk you accepted. |
| The management company has an operational failure | The funds are separate legal pools. A disaster-recovery plan is not the same as a guarantee. |
| A broker fails | **SIPC** covers brokerage custody up to statutory limits. It does not insure market losses. This statute was **not** reopened for this edit. |
| A bank fails | **FDIC** covers deposits up to the legal limit. It does not cover VTSAX. |

Collins’s statement that Vanguard does not pay him is his disclosure. It was not independently audited here.

## Chapter 19. Which account

The container is not the investment. A 401(k), 403(b), Thrift Savings Plan, IRA, or Roth IRA is a tax wrapper around funds you choose.

### 2026 contribution limits

Figures below are the 2026 limits published from IRS tables. [11]

| Account | 2026 limit |
| --- | --- |
| Employee deferral: 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b), TSP | $24,500 |
| Catch-up, age 50 and older | $8,000 |
| Catch-up, ages 60 through 63 | $11,250 |
| Employee plus employer, before catch-up | $72,000 |
| Traditional IRA and Roth IRA, combined | $7,500 |
| IRA catch-up, age 50 and older | $1,100 |

Roth IRA income phaseouts for 2026:

- single or head of household: **$153,000 to $168,000**;
- married filing jointly: **$242,000 to $252,000**.

In 2026, catch-up deferrals must be **Roth** for a worker whose prior-year wages from that employer exceeded **$150,000**. [11]

The book’s 2016 limits of **$18,000** and **$24,000** are history. The case-study line that cites a **2025** IRA limit of **$6,500** is wrong for both 2025 and 2026.

### A workable order

1. Take the full employer match.
2. Fund a Roth IRA while your bracket is low and you are allowed to contribute.
3. Use a deductible traditional IRA when that deduction is worth more than the Roth.
4. Finish the workplace plan.
5. Only then use a taxable account.

A nondeductible IRA is mainly useful as part of a backdoor Roth. The pro-rata rule still bites if you already hold pretax IRA money.

When you leave a job, rolling a costly 401(k) or 403(b) into an IRA is still reasonable. The TSP has often been cheap enough that **leaving the money there** is the better choice. Its current expense ratios were not pulled.

### Roth, traditional, and heirs

- Roth accounts still have **no required distribution** for the original owner.
- Since the SECURE Act, many non-spouse heirs must empty an inherited IRA within **10 years**. That replaced the old “stretch IRA.”
- A traditional contribution is a deduction now and ordinary income later.
- A Roth contribution is tax now, and qualified withdrawals later are tax-free.
- Which one wins depends on a future bracket you do not know.

Congress can change the rules. A national sales tax that would tax spending from a Roth account is still a **hypothetical**, not law.

### 2026 rate brackets

After the 2025 tax law that kept the post-2017 rate structure and adjusted it for inflation, the ordinary brackets are: [12]

`10% · 12% · 22% · 24% · 32% · 35% · 37%`

| Threshold (taxable income) | Single | Married filing jointly |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 22% bracket begins above | $50,400 | $100,800 |
| 37% bracket begins above | $640,600 | $768,700 |
| Standard deduction | $16,100 | $32,200 |

The talk’s **15 percent** and **25 percent** brackets are gone.

## Chapter 20. Required distributions

Required minimum distributions are **not** triggered at 70½, and they are **not** universally triggered at 73.

| Born | Age when RMDs start |
| --- | --- |
| 1951 through 1959 | 73 |
| 1960 or later | 75 |

The first deadline can be **1 April** of the following year. Using that grace period stacks two distributions into one tax year. Most people are better off taking the first distribution in the year they reach the age.

On the Uniform Lifetime Table, the divisor at **73** is **26.5**, about **3.8 percent** of the prior **31 December** balance. [13]

Roth IRAs still have no lifetime RMD for the owner.

### The penalty changed

| Rule | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Old penalty in the talk | 50% of the missed RMD |
| Current penalty | **25%** of the shortfall |
| If you correct it inside the window | **10%** |

That is still a penalty to avoid. [13]

### The old tax example does not describe 2026

The chapter’s sample dollar path, and the retired couple filling a **15 percent** bracket up to **$96,000**, do not describe current law.

A married couple in the **12 percent** bracket in 2026 hits **22 percent** above **$100,800** of taxable income, before the standard deduction is applied. [12]

The tactic is intact. In low-income years **before** RMDs, convert slices of a traditional IRA to a Roth while the bracket is known and modest.

Inherited-IRA rules belong in the same conversation. A child who inherits a traditional IRA may have to withdraw it within a decade, which can collide with that child’s peak earning years.

## Chapter 21. Health savings accounts

An HSA is still the only common account that is:

1. deductible on the way in;
2. untaxed as it grows;
3. untaxed on the way out, when the withdrawal pays a qualified medical expense.

### 2026 HSA limits

| Item | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Self-only contribution | $4,400 |
| Family contribution | $8,750 |
| Catch-up at age 55 or older | $1,000 |
| Minimum HDHP deductible, self-only | $1,700 |
| Minimum HDHP deductible, family | $3,400 |

Source: 2026 IRS figures. [11]

The talk’s “about $3,000 and $7,750” is behind.

### The long-game use

- Pay small current bills from cash if you can.
- Save the receipts.
- Leave the HSA invested in a broad index fund.
- Reimburse yourself later. The IRS does not set a short deadline on a documented qualified expense.
- After **65**, a non-medical withdrawal is taxed like a traditional IRA, without the extra penalty.

A flexible spending account is **not** an HSA. FSAs are mostly use-it-or-lose-it, with a limited carryover or grace period. Read your own plan. The carryover dollar cap was **not** rechecked.

## Chapter 22. A case study

The 26-year-old is a teaching sketch: a **$70,000** salary, no debt, a **2.5 percent** match, and **$35,000** from grandparents.

In 2026 the order is:

1. Take the match.
2. Fund a Roth IRA up to **$7,500** if income qualifies. [11]
3. Decide how much more goes into the 403(b) versus a taxable brokerage account.

The talk’s **$5,500** and **$6,500** IRA caps are retired numbers.

Selling twelve advisor funds can realize capital gains. That tax is often smaller than a lifetime of high fees. Invest each paycheck as it arrives. Do not wait for a dip you cannot schedule.

> Lifestyle inflation is the silent killer. When pay rises, raise the savings rate before you raise the rent.

## Chapter 23. Advisors

Commission loads still exist. A **5.75 percent** front load is no longer “very common” in the old sense, but it is not extinct. Annuities and cash-value life insurance still pay large commissions.

| Way the advisor is paid | Conflict |
| --- | --- |
| Load or commission | The product that pays the advisor may be the product that costs you most. |
| Assets under management, often about 1% | The advisor is paid more if you keep money invested, even when paying off debt would serve you better. |
| Hourly | The cleanest of the three. You can still receive bad advice. |
| A blend of all three | You will not see the total easily. |

An assets-under-management fee of **1 percent** is ordinary. **Two percent** is hard to justify.

The talk’s compound-growth illustration of a 2 percent fee is arithmetically garbled: the ending balances do not match its own 11.9 percent assumption. The direction is not garbled. A 1 to 2 percent annual fee, stacked on fund expenses, removes a large share of the spending money a 4 percent withdrawal was supposed to provide.

Bernie Madoff remains the extreme case of handing over control. He is not the typical case. The typical case is a pleasant person who costs you a quiet fortune.

## Chapter 24. Jack Bogle

The first retail S&P 500 index fund arrived in the **mid-1970s**. Wall Street mocked it. It won on cost.

Bogle died in **2019**. [1] Vanguard is no longer the only low-cost shop, and it is not the only place you should be willing to hold a cheap index fund.

Buffett’s instruction — that the money left for his wife go mostly into a low-cost S&P 500 index fund — is still the cleanest public endorsement this idea has.

| Ticker | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| `VTSAX` / `VTI` | Total U.S. stock market |
| `VFIAX` / `VOO` | S&P 500 only |

The talk sometimes swaps those names. Either is a reasonable core. They are **not** the same portfolio. The S&P 500 leaves out most smaller companies. The total market includes them, and in 2026 it is still dominated at the top by the largest firms. [4]

## Chapter 25. You will not pick the winners

Professionals with more information than you still fail to beat the index after fees. [8]

*The Intelligent Investor* was written before a retail index fund existed. Graham’s later sympathy for indexing, and Buffett’s advice for ordinary investors, point the same way.

Insider trading is still illegal. Most of what feels like an edge is a forecast stacked on another forecast.

Own the market instead.

## Chapter 26. Lump sums versus dollar-cost averaging

Investing each paycheck **is** dollar-cost averaging. You do it because the money arrives over time.

Spreading a lump sum you **already hold** is a different decision. It is a bet that the market will fall while you wait. Markets have risen in more calendar years than they have fallen. The talk’s “33 of 43 years from 1970 to 2013” was **not** re-counted. The newer record, including 2020 and 2022, does not reverse the tendency.

If a lump-sum investment will make you sell at the first drop, averaging in is a behavioral compromise. It is not the higher-expected-value move.

Once the money is in, a later crash can still happen. Averaging did not insure you.

## Chapter 27. Gurus

Louis Rukeyser’s *Wall Street Week*, Abby Joseph Cohen’s permanent bullishness, and Marty Zweig’s permanent worry are period pieces. The method of becoming a television sage has not changed.

1. Make a dated, extreme call.
2. Stay quiet when it fails.
3. Repeat until one call matches a real crash or a real boom.
4. Issue the press release.

CNBC and YouTube did not invent this. They scaled it.

The useful response is to turn the forecast off and automate the index purchase.

## Chapter 28. You can be conned

The person who says “I cannot be conned” is the mark.

### The stock-tip funnel

A promoter sends a tip to a large list.

- Half are told the stock will rise.
- Half are told it will fall.
- Next week, only the people who saw a “correct” call get another tip.

By the sixth round, a small room of people has watched him be right every time. They were selected by arithmetic, not by skill.

### Five rules

1. Everyone is vulnerable. Confidence makes you a target.
2. You are often **most** vulnerable inside your own field. Madoff took finance professionals.
3. Con artists look trustworthy. That is the job.
4. Most of a good lie is true. The hook is in the part that is brushed past.
5. If it sounds too good to be true, it is.

Index funds matter here for a grim family reason. A spouse or an adult child can understand them after you are gone. A portfolio of stories cannot.

## Chapter 29. How much you can spend

The Trinity study in the late 1990s found that a balanced portfolio withdrawing **4 percent**, then adjusted for inflation, lasted **30 years** in the great majority of historical U.S. periods.

Collins’s retelling — a 50/50 mix, about a **96 percent** success rate, with bad starts in the mid-1960s — matches the popular summary of that work. It was **not** re-simulated for this update.

### What 4 percent is

$$
4\% \times \$1{,}000{,}000 = \$40{,}000 \text{ in the first year}
$$

Later years in the classic rule are adjusted for inflation. The portfolio is not a contract to pay you that sum.

| Stance | Withdrawal | Who it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Very cautious | About 3% | Fixed bills, little flexibility, long retirement |
| Classic anchor | 4% | The Trinity planning number |
| Flexible | 5% to 6% | People who will cut spending when markets fall |

A rigid 4 percent **plus** a 1 percent fee is a different and worse plan. The book’s claim that a 1 percent fee drops success to **84 percent**, and a 2 percent fee to **65 percent**, was **not** re-run.

Collins’s own “a bit over 5 percent, because college and travel are flexible, and Social Security is coming” is his life at the time of writing. It is not a 2026 instruction. It is not evidence that 5 percent is safe for a retiree with fixed bills and no other income.

## Chapter 30. How to pull the 4 percent

Twenty-five times annual spending is the same statement as 4 percent.

$$
\frac{1}{0.04} = 25
$$

### Where the shares sit

- Bonds in the traditional IRA, when you can, because interest is taxed every year in a taxable account.
- Stocks in the Roth IRA and in the taxable account.
- Treat every account as **one portfolio** when you measure the mix.

### A spending order

1. Spend earned income first, if you still have any.
2. Sell unwanted legacy holdings slowly, worst ones first, watching the tax bill.
3. Spend taxable-account dividends. You are taxed on them anyway.
4. Sell taxable shares when the dividends are not enough.
5. Before RMDs, convert traditional IRA money to Roth inside a bracket you can see.
6. After RMDs begin, at **73 or 75** depending on birth year, those withdrawals can be the spending. [13]

The talk’s “age 70½” and “age 75 for everyone” are both wrong as a single rule.

### Do not budget on the book’s yields

| Fund | Talk’s yield | What turned up in this check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| VTSAX | About 2% | Closer to **1%** in the Morningstar data reviewed. [4] |
| VBTLX | About 3% | About **5%** on a Vanguard advisor page in October 2026, after the post-2022 rate reset. Yields fall when bond prices rise. [10] |

Check the current yield. Do not automate 4 percent and then ignore a 50 percent decline. Revisit the plan at least once a year, and after any move of about 20 percent.

## Chapter 31. Social Security

The program began in **1935**. Collins’s early-adult assumption that it would vanish was a planning choice, not a description of the law.

### The 2026 Trustees Report

| Item | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| Combined OASI and DI reserves at the end of 2025 | **$2.56 trillion** (down $160 billion that year) |
| Cost versus total income | Cost has been higher **since 2021** |
| Combined reserves projected to last until | **2034** |
| Share of scheduled benefits payable then | About **83%** |
| Retirement fund (OASI) alone | Shortfall in the **fourth quarter of 2032**, about **78%** payable |
| Disability reserves | Projected to last through the 75-year window |

Those reserves are Treasury bonds. They are real obligations. They are not a pile of cash in a drawer. [14]

The talk’s “$2.7 trillion, empty in 2033, 75 percent payable” is the **old** path.

### When to claim

| Age | What you get |
| --- | --- |
| 62 | The earliest check. It is permanently smaller. |
| Full retirement age | 67 for anyone born in **1960 or later** |
| 70 | Delayed credits stop. The worker’s check is larger. A surviving spouse can step up to it. |

The break-even “around 84” is a common planning shorthand, not a personal calculation. Collins’s plan to wait until 70 is **his** plan.

Higher payroll taxes, a later full retirement age, or means-testing for younger workers are **possible legislation**. They are not current law. Do not write them into a spreadsheet as if they had passed.

The combined Social Security and Medicare payroll tax is still **15.3 percent**, split between worker and employer, before the additional Medicare tax on high earners. The Social Security wage base changes every year and was not copied here.

Planning as if the benefit will be small, then treating whatever arrives as a bonus, is still the conservative stance.

## Chapter 32. Giving

Charitable gifts reduce taxable income **if you itemize**.

With a 2026 standard deduction of **$32,200** for a married couple, many households will not itemize. A small annual gift may have **no** federal income-tax value. [12]

Two tools still solve that:

- **Bunching**, or giving appreciated shares to a donor-advised fund in a high-income year.
- A **qualified charitable distribution** from an IRA, available at **70½**, which can also satisfy an RMD.

Vanguard Charitable was Collins’s vehicle. Its current account minimum was **not verified**. Done correctly, the tax shape he describes is still available: a deduction in the year you fund the gift, no capital-gains tax on appreciated shares you donate, and grants to charities later.

> You do not owe your money to a phone solicitor. A gift is a gift because you chose it.

Giving cash to a neighbor may not be deductible. It can still be the right use of the money.

## Chapter 33. A ten-year plan

This is the advice Collins says he gave his daughter. It is not only for a 22-year-old.

1. Treat consumer debt as poison.
2. Choose a partner and a circle who will not undo the plan.
3. Treat the early career years as skill-building.
4. Do not raise spending in lockstep with raises.
5. Save a large share — half, if you actually can — into the match, the workplace plan up to the current limit, a Roth IRA while you qualify, and a total-market fund.
6. Treat market declines, while you are still buying, as sales.
7. Call yourself independent when investments are about **25 times** spending.
8. Then keep working, cut back, or change the work. The paycheck is optional.
9. A house is a consumption decision unless the numbers say otherwise.
10. Starting at 55 still helps. It does not recreate a 22-year-old’s timeline.

| Annual spending | Portfolio at 25× |
| --- | ---: |
| $20,000 | $500,000 |
| $40,000 | $1,000,000 |
| $80,000 | $2,000,000 |

The transferable piece is that money is for a life you chose. The vehicle in this book stays dull on purpose:

## Chapter 35. Risk

The Dow began the twentieth century in the double digits and ended that century near **11,500**. It closed **16 October 2026** at **52,002.41**. [7]

That path includes wars, inflation, and crashes. It is **one country’s** history.

| Asset | How it feels | What it has done over long U.S. stretches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash | Calm | Loses purchasing power |
| Stocks | Violent | Built purchasing power, with deep interruptions |
| A forecast | Certain | Usually expires on contact with the next year |

Volatility is the turbulence. Selling at the bottom is the crash.

You do not get a risk-free choice. You pick which risk to hold.

Nothing here guarantees that the next 25 years will resemble the last 50, or that 4 percent will survive every future sequence of returns. The plan is a bet on three things:

1. a growing economy of real businesses;
2. costs low enough that fees do not eat the withdrawal;
3. your own behavior when the headline is loud.

- [ ] Start.
- [ ] Automate the investment.
- [ ] Leave it alone when the news is performing.
